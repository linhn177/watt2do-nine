Battery and e-waste risks

About 10 kg

Average lead content in a lead-acid battery, according to WHO

Lead from improperly handled batteries can contaminate soil and dust. Children can be exposed by swallowing or breathing contaminated dust. WHO says no level of lead exposure is known to be harmless.

WHO lead poisoning guidance

245 fires

Fires identified at 64 U.S. waste facilities, 2013–2020

EPA identified fires caused or likely caused by lithium-ion or lithium-metal batteries. This is a count of documented incidents across the facilities studied.

EPA battery fire report

About 50 days

Time until leaching in one laboratory water test

Guo and colleagues immersed spent NiCd batteries in four types of water. They observed leaching in about 50 days at the tested salt concentration and in under 100 days in the other samples. Timing depends on the test conditions.

Guo et al. (2018)

As little as 2 hours

Time for severe burns if a battery lodges in the esophagus

Large lithium coin cells, around 20 mm, can lodge in a child's esophagus. The battery can create an electrical current that burns tissue. A study of 56,535 reported battery ingestions found the share of button battery ingestions with serious or fatal outcomes rose 6.7-fold from 1985 to 2009. If you suspect a child swallowed a button battery, seek emergency care immediately.

Litovitz et al. (2010)

Seawater reached pH 11 in 30 days

In a lab test with whole, spent alkaline AA batteries

Seawater
pH 11
Landfill leachate
pH 10

Researchers submerged whole, spent AA batteries in rainwater, seawater, or landfill leachate and tested the liquids after 1, 7, and 30 days. After 30 days with alkaline AA batteries, the seawater sample reached pH 11 and the landfill leachate sample reached pH 10. The seawater results indicated that electrolyte had leaked from the cells. The rainwater results suggested little leakage of internal components under the test conditions.

Câmara et al. (2012), Química Nova

Had blood lead levels above 10 micrograms per deciliter

A 2024 study of 327 volunteers at and around the informal e-waste recycling site in Agbogbloshie, Ghana, reported this finding. Unsafe dismantling can release lead into dust and expose workers and people nearby.

Lead exposure can harm the nervous system. Children are especially vulnerable.
